Your Own Dates: a plain-English manual

Who this is for. You have some dates from your own life. You want to put them into Ophis, press the button, and understand what comes back. You do not want to read a specification first.

That is this document. No maths background assumed. Ten minutes to your first result, and everything you need to read it honestly. Every click below was driven in the real app, and every number quoted is pinned by the test suite.

If you want the theory instead, the field guide is the ten-minute tour and the white paper is the study.

1. What the thing actually does

Give it two or more dates on which the same kind of thing happened to you.

It measures the gap between every pair of those dates, in whole days. Then it takes each gap and runs it through sixteen small arithmetic formulas — halve it, multiply it by 1.618, reverse its digits, and so on. Each result is a number of days, which it counts forward from one of your dates to land on a new date.

That is the whole idea. Your dates go in; a ranked list of projected dates comes out. The score next to each one says how many independent formulas agreed on it and whether the gap it came from is a number the system treats as significant.

It knows nothing about you, your event, or the world. It does arithmetic on the numbers you typed.

2. The one rule that decides whether you get anything useful

The dates must be repetitions of the same kind of event.

This is the rule people break, and breaking it makes everything downstream meaningless — the arithmetic still runs, the scores still appear, and they are noise.

The instrument's own word for what you are feeding it is an Iso-Event: an event that has already happened two or more times and might happen again. The whole method assumes the gaps between past occurrences carry a pattern. Three unrelated milestones have no pattern between them to find.

Good inputWhy
The three times you changed jobsSame event, repeated
Every time you have moved houseSame event, repeated
Each time a chronic condition flared upSame event, repeated
The dates of every major argument in one relationshipSame event, repeated
Every time your business had a record monthSame event, repeated
Bad inputWhy
Your birthday, your wedding, your graduationThree different events
One dateNothing to measure a gap against
Two dates one week apartOne tiny gap; every projection lands in a heap
Dates you half-rememberThe gap is the whole input; a month out is garbage in
Dates you chose to be evenly spacedThe formulas agree by construction (§8)

Two dates is the minimum. Three is where it gets interesting — three dates give three gaps instead of one, so three times as much to work with.

If you cannot think of an event that has happened to you three or more times and whose dates you actually know, this instrument has nothing to tell you. That is a real answer, not a failure.

3. Ten minutes: your first run

Open the app (index.html). It starts on the Work screen with a worked example already loaded, so you can see the shape of things before you touch it.

Step 1 — Name your event. In the Iso-Events panel on the left, click the name field next to E1 and type what your dates have in common. "Started a new job". "Moved house". The name changes nothing in the maths; it stops you forgetting what you were asking six months from now.

Step 2 — Clear the example dates. In the X-Dates panel, press Delete All and confirm with Yes, delete all. (Or remove rows one at a time with their ✕.) X-Dates is what the app calls your input dates — X for the ones you know, Z for the ones it projects.

Step 3 — Add your dates. Type into the MON, DAY and YEAR boxes at the bottom of the X-Dates panel and press + X-Date for each one. You can add them in any order: + X-Date slots each new date into its place in time, so the list always reads oldest to newest.

Editing a date in place is different, and it is the one way to break a run. Each row is also a text box you can retype (MM/DD/YYYY, then Enter). That edit is taken literally. Retype X1 to a date later than X2 and the run stops: the status bar drops to 0 PAIRS, the table empties, and the panel reports X2 must be greater than X1. The list must climb by at least a day, row by row — X1 means the first row, not the earliest date. Fix the row and it casts again. If you need to change a date a lot, deleting it and re-adding it with + X-Date is the safe way.

Step 4 — Read the status bar. Across the top you will see something like 3 ANCHORS · 3 PAIRS · 16 OPERATIONS · 23 Z-DATES · 24 HIDDEN, then a Current date box, a state label, and Recalculate.

  • PAIRS 0 means fewer than two dates are enabled — or an ordering error (Step 3).
  • Current date is the day the projection is "being conducted from". It starts as today. Change it (type MM/DD/YYYY, Enter) to see what the run would have looked like on another day; it matters to one filter (§9) and to Protocol Prime (§7). It is not saved in a file.
  • The app re-casts on every change, so the label normally reads Up to date. Recalculate forces a fresh cast — useful if the tab has been open across midnight and "today" has moved.

Step 5 — Read the table. It is sorted by date. Click the Score column header to sort by score instead, highest first. That is your shortlist.

Step 6 — Click a row. Any row opens the Audit screen: the score worked out line by line, every formula that landed there with its anchor, gap and result, and the full list of gaps (Y-structs) the run used. If a number ever surprises you, this is where you find out why — nothing is hidden.

That is a complete run. Everything below is about reading it well.

4. Reading a row

A row looks like this:

Z8   05/18/2024   ♊   2   2   —   O1(X2→X3) O2(X2→X3)

Z8 — the row's number in date order. It stays put when you re-sort, so you can refer to "Z8" while the list is sorted by score.

Z-DATE — the projected date. This is the output.

The glyph — the hit count again, as a shape so it does not rely on colour: ♊ two, ▲ three, ◆ four, ● five or more. Blank means one.

HITS — how many separate things pointed at this date. It counts both the formulas that landed here and the significant numbers that matched. More hits means more independent agreement, and independent agreement is the entire point of the method. This is the column to trust most.

SCORE — hits, weighted. Explained in full in §5.

MSRF — a green, red or purple chip showing a number, or —. MSRF stands for Multidimensional Spatial Recognition Filters, which is a long name for a list of 390 numbers the system treats as significant. If a gap behind this date is on that list, you get a chip. Green is Normal, red is Important, purple is Vortex. Red and purple are rarer and count for more. Hover a chip for detail.

A — in the MSRF column is completely normal. Most rows have nothing there.

OPERATIONS — which formulas landed here, and from which pair of your dates. O5(X2→X4) means formula number 5, working on the gap between your second and fourth date. The Operations screen lists all sixteen with their equations.

The Columns buttons above the table hide or show each column.

5. How the score is built

The score is:

score = (formula points + number points) × multiplier

Formula points. Each formula that lands on the date contributes its weight. The sixteen shipped formulas are split into two classes: Alpha ones are worth 1 point, Beta ones 0.5. So two Beta formulas agreeing gives 1 point; two Alpha formulas gives 2.

Number points and the multiplier. If the gap matches the significant-number list, that match brings both points and a multiplier:

ChipPointsMultiplier
Normal (green)1×1.5
Important (red)2×2
Vortex (purple)2×2

The multiplier is the largest one on the row — they never stack. And the match that supplies the multiplier does not also pay its points, on the grounds that it is already being counted once. No match at all means a multiplier of ×1, which changes nothing.

6. Is my score any good?

This is the question everyone actually has, and it has a real answer. Here is how the 114 rows of the worked example are distributed:

ScoreRowsWhat that means
0.529The floor. One Beta formula, nothing else
0.7513
1.053The single most common outcome
1.512Now something is agreeing
2.05Top 6% of rows
2.251
3.01The single best row in the whole run

Read that table before you get excited about a number.

  • 1.0 or below is 83% of everything. It means one formula landed there and nothing agreed. It is not a signal. It is the background.
  • 1.5 to 2.0 is where a row starts to be worth a second look.
  • Above 2 is rare — two rows out of 114 in a full run with five dates.

A score is only meaningful relative to the other rows in your own run. A 2.0 in a run whose best row is 2.0 is your top result. A 2.0 in a run with three 3.0s is mid-table. Always sort by score and look at the shape of the whole column before deciding any single row means something.

7. Protocol Prime — the step the software never tells you

The author's own procedure adds a third kind of control that nothing in the original software hints at: the date you are doing the projection on.

Not just your historical dates — today's date, entered as another X-Date. The reasoning is that the moment you ask the question is itself part of the pattern.

There is a button for it: ☉ Today · Protocol Prime in the X-Dates panel. It adds the Current date (§3, Step 4) as a control, slotted into date order like any other + X-Date, and says "Today is already a control" if you press it twice.

What it does to the numbers. Adding a fourth date takes you from 3 pairs to 6, which doubles the projections. Here are the job dates of §8 run both ways, with the Current date held at 09/02/2026 for both — the comparison only means something if that is the same on each row:

PairsProjectionsShownHidden
Three job dates348641
+ today6962570

(Shown plus Hidden counts distinct days, and comes to one less than the projections cast: in each run, one projection lands on a day another already occupies, and the two merge into a single row.)

Six rows becomes twenty-five, and most of that is not the extra projections. For the job dates, today is later than all of them, so Protocol Prime makes it the last X-Date — and that slides the "beyond 2,559 days from the last X-Date" filter more than four years forward. The window moved; the arithmetic barely changed.

And look at what arrived. The top four rows are the same four dates in both runs — 09/29/2026, 01/21/2027, 02/15/2027, 11/10/2027 — each scoring 1.00, and not one row in either run carries an MSRF chip. The nineteen new rows all score 1.00 or 0.50.

So: more input gave a longer list and an identical top. More input does not automatically mean more signal, and a list getting longer is not the same thing as it getting better. Open 03-job-changes-protocol-prime.oph to see the second run.

8. When nothing lights up

Here is a real run on three real-shaped job dates — 14 Mar 2016, 6 Sep 2019, 11 Jan 2022 — cast with the Current date set to 02/01/2022, just after the last of them. (That setting matters: leave the Current date at today and the hide before the current date filter cuts this run down to six rows, because everything below already happened. Set it to reproduce what you see here — or open 02-job-changes.oph, which has that filter off and shows these rows on any day.)

3 anchors | 3 pairs | 48 projections | 47 distinct dates | 23 shown | 24 hidden

Top rows:
  05/18/2024   score 2   hits 2   MSRF: none
  06/30/2022   score 1   hits 1   MSRF: none
  02/28/2023   score 1   hits 1   MSRF: none

Top score of 2 — two Alpha formulas landing on one day, with no help from the number list — and not a single significant-number match in the entire run.

This is the normal outcome, and it is the honest one. The instrument is not broken and you did not do it wrong. Three arbitrary real-world dates mostly do not produce gaps that land on a list of 390 particular numbers, and there is no reason they should.

If you want the tool to tell you something, that has to be a thing it is allowed to not do. A version that always found a strong signal would be finding it in noise.

Things worth trying before you conclude there is nothing:

  • Check your dates. The gap in days is the entire input. One wrong date poisons every projection derived from it.
  • Add a fourth occurrence if you have one. More pairs, more chances.
  • Try Protocol Prime (§7).
  • Loosen the filters (§9) — the default settings are hiding rows.

Why evenly spaced dates light up — and why that proves nothing

Now the opposite. Three dates exactly 138 days apart, starting on the 19th: 01/19/2019, 06/06/2019, 10/22/2019 (04-even-spacing-19-138.oph).

3 anchors | 3 pairs | 32 shown | 5 hidden

Top row:
  06/01/2020   score 4.5   hits 4   MSRF: two green chips (223)

A 4.5 is higher than anything in the app's own worked example. It means nothing, and the Audit screen shows why. The gaps are 138 and 276, and 276 is exactly 2 × 138. One formula takes half a gap × 1.618; another takes a gap × 1.618. Half of 276 is 138, so both formulas compute 138 × 1.618 = 223.3, from the same last date, and land on the same day. 223 happens to be on the Normal list, so the agreement is scored twice over.

The formulas did not discover a pattern; the pattern was typed in. Evenly spaced input makes the sixteen formulas collide by construction. Real events are rarely that regular — and when yours are, that regularity is the thing to notice, not the score built on top of it. Treat any run whose gaps are simple multiples of each other with suspicion.

9. The filters, and why rows go missing

24 HIDDEN on the status bar means exactly that: 24 projections were computed and then removed before you saw them. The Filters panel controls this. There are eight, numbered F1–F8, and they only ever remove rows — none of them can invent one. Ticked means the filter is on.

FilterDefault
F1Hide Z-Dates before the last X-Dateon
F2Hide Z-Dates on the last X-Dateon
F3Hide Z-Dates before the current dateon
F4Hide Z-Dates on the current dateoff
F5Hide Z-Dates beyond n days from the last X-Dateon, 2559
F6Hide Z-Dates with fewer than n hitsoff, 2
F7Hide Z-Dates scoring below noff, 1
F8Hide Z-Dates with no MSRF matchoff

The ones you will care about:

  • F3 — before the current date. Usually right — a projection into last year is not a forecast. It is the only filter that reads the clock, which is why the files in examples/ ship with it off: switch it off and a run shows the same rows on any day. Turn it off yourself to check the method against something that already happened.
  • **F6 — fewer than n hits.** The most useful dial in the app. Tick it (the value is already 2) and everything with no agreement disappears at once.
  • **F7 — scoring below n.** The same idea, by score instead of hits.
  • F8 — no MSRF match. Aggressive. Turns 114 rows into a handful. Worth trying once to see what survives.
  • **F5 — beyond n days.** Caps how far out it will look. 2559 days is the author's window — about seven years.

Press Reset in that panel to get back to the defaults.

A useful habit: run once with the filters wide open to see the true spread, then tighten them. Starting tight can hide the fact that your top row is only top because everything else was cut.

T-Dates are the opposite tool. If you only care about particular days — the next scheduled match, a planned launch — add them in the T-Dates panel and the table keeps only Z-Dates that land exactly on one of them. Leave it empty (the default, "showing every date") to see everything.

10. The rest of the screen

The timeline. Every arc jumps forward from the X-Date it was counted from to the date it projects; its colour is the hit count (grey 1–2, yellow 3, blue 4, red 5+). Hover an arc to measure it, use the wheel to zoom, drag to pan, and Recentre to reset.

Chart Config. Overlays for the timeline: C1–C2 turn the chart and its date labels on and off; C3–C10 mark the eight moon phases; C11–C14 mark solar and lunar eclipses (full and partial). They draw context on the chart and never change a score.

Scope & Location. Days (the default) works in whole days, locked to UTC so the same file gives the same answer on any machine. HH:MM works to the minute; only then does a location matter, and ◍ Pick on map sets it from the offline map.

More than one event. + Add in the Iso-Events panel starts another event (E2, E3…); the radio button chooses which one the screen shows, ⧉ clones one, ✕ deletes it. Each event keeps its own dates, filters and settings. The Transfer screen copies chosen parts — X-Dates, Filters, T-Dates, Operations and so on — from one event onto others.

Operations. The sixteen formulas, editable. Untick one to leave it out of the cast; change its weight to move it between Alpha (1 or more) and Beta. Changing these changes every score, so note what you did.

Settings. S1 Misc. Notes is free text that never affects results — the example files use it to say what you should see. S2 sets the hour a day begins in Days scope.

11. Saving, files and exports

Your work is kept in this browser. Close the tab and come back, and your events are still there. But it is kept only in this browser on this machine (at this web address) — a different browser, device or private window starts fresh, and clearing site data erases it.

The badge at the top right reads Not saved until you export a file, and Saved after. It is about files, not the browser copy.

To keep a run for good: Export → Export file. That writes an .oph file (plain text; the Export screen also shows it for Copy). Save for Ophis v12 writes the older format the original desktop app reads — the plain export opens there with no X-Dates.

To bring one back: Import → ☰ Open .oph file… → Load, or paste the text and press Load. Nothing changes until you press Load, and if you have unsaved changes it asks before overwriting them. The Current date is not part of a file: an imported run is cast against today (§9, F3).

To take the results elsewhere: the Z-Dates screen exports the table as CSV, XLSX or PDF. All three honour your filters and list rows by date, whatever column you last sorted on.

Nothing leaves your machine. No network call, no account, no telemetry.

12. What the score is not

Worth being straight about, because the output looks authoritative and dates feel personal.

It is not a prediction. It is the result of fixed arithmetic on the numbers you typed. The date has no causal relationship to your life; it is a gap multiplied by 1.618 and added to a day you supplied.

It is not evidence. A high score means several formulas in one table happened to converge. With 160 projections from five dates, some convergence is expected by chance alone — and with evenly spaced dates it is guaranteed (§8). That is why §6 matters: the question is never "is this score high" but "is it high compared to the rest of this run".

It does not know what your event was. Job, illness, argument — identical arithmetic. The name you typed is a label for you.

Do not make a decision you could not otherwise justify. If the tool points at a date and you find yourself planning around it, the thing doing the work is your own attention, not the arithmetic. Use it to notice a period worth watching, not to decide anything that matters on its own.

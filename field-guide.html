Two dates in, a shortlist out.

Ophis takes dates on which similar events happened before, measures the distances between them, and projects those distances forward. Everything else it does is throwing away the results that bear no arithmetic relationship to what you gave it.

Ten minutes end to end. You need no maths beyond arithmetic.

One

Three letters do all the work

X-Date
A control. A date in the past when the event you care about actually happened. You supply these; the instrument supplies nothing.
Y
The distance between two controls, counted in whole days — in the author's phrase, in axial rotations of the Earth.
Z-Date
A projection. One operation applied to one Y, added to one of the two controls that produced it.

Sixteen operations run against every pair of controls. Five controls make ten pairs, so five dates in gives 160 projections out — which collapse to fewer distinct days, because different operations often land on the same one. That collapsing is the first signal.

Two

What the operations actually do

Every operation is an ordinary arithmetic expression starting X1+ or X2+, which decides which of the two controls the result is added to. The rest is a function of Y.

The first three operations, on Y = 47
O1X2+oph_round(Y)47→ 10/06/2026
O2X2+oph_flip(oph_round(Y))74→ 11/02/2026
O3X2+Y/OPH_CRV9.25→ 08/29/2026

oph_flip reverses the digits: 47 becomes 74. OPH_CRV is 5.08, one of four constants the engine ships. Both are the author's, and neither is negotiable if you want the numbers the original produced.

Alpha and Beta. Each operation carries a weight. At or above 1 it is an Alpha projection, below it a Beta. The author's own note: the Alpha projections are "future dates that are most likely to produce events that mirror the events of X-1 and X-2." The weight is not a label — it is added to the score directly.

Three

The MSRF — which projections to believe

Sixteen operations across ten pairs produce a lot of dates, and most of them mean nothing. The Multidimensional Spatial Recognition Filter is how the instrument decides which to keep: it takes the distance from each projection back to its control and looks that number up in three lists.

Those 390 numbers are the compressed output of six separate filters the author built — Annular Chronometric, Geodetic Chronometry, Hydrogen Spacing Angle, Fibonacci Sequence, IntraDecimal Matrix and Vortex Holography. Only the resulting numbers ever reached the software.

Normal

The broad set. A match is worth one point.

325 numbers · ×1.5
Important

A smaller, stronger set — highly composite numbers, mostly. Worth two points.

53 numbers · ×2.0
Vortex

Twelve numbers, and the only ones with a decimal. The author found them by subtracting every number from 1 to 15 000 from its own digit-reversal and keeping the ~5 % that never collapse to zero.

12 numbers · ×2.0 · checked first

Order matters more than you would expect. Vortex is checked before anything else, so 43.5 matches even though half-day values are otherwise ignored — because 43.5 is a vortex number. And 76.3 matches Vortex 76.2, stealing what would have been a plain Normal 76 and doubling its weight.

Four

Reading one row

Here is the strongest row from the worked example the instrument opens with — five controls spanning a year, cast forward.

date

204.1 → Normal 204  ·  74.4 → Normal 74

max(1.5, 1.5) = 1.5
subscore
2 points, minus the one carrying the multiplier  →  1.0
base
1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0
score
2.0 × 1.5 = 3
hits
2 operations + 2 resonance = 4

The rule worth internalising: the strongest resonance match multiplies rather than adds, and is withheld from the points sum so it is not paid for twice. The multiplier is a maximum, never a product — ten Important matches still multiply once.

Two operations agreeing matters more than one scoring well. A date several different operations independently land on is the thing the whole instrument exists to surface. Sort by Hits, not only by Score.

Five

Protocol Prime

This is the part nothing in the software tells you, and it triples what you get out.

The author's flow chart takes three controls, not two: the two historical dates, and the date on which you are running the projection. That third date produces two more distances — one to each of the others — and every operation runs against all three.

Fourteen operations across three spans is the forty-two future dates the author's chart describes, collapsing to "38 or 39 actual dates" once duplicates merge. Enter only two controls and you see a third of the picture.

Add today's date as a third X-Date. That is the whole of it. The engine forms every pair automatically, so the two extra spans appear on their own.

The author described Protocol Prime as a security measure — that an unauthorised user "can not use system to predict future events" without it. Read plainly, an operator who enters only two dates is simply not running the method.

Six

Cutting the noise

Eight filters trim the output, and each can only ever remove rows. Three are on by default and do most of the work: hide anything before your last control, hide anything before today, and hide anything more than 2 559 days out.

Two more are worth reaching for once you have a feel for a data set. Minimum hits at 3 keeps only dates that several operations agreed on. Requires an MSRF match keeps only dates that resonate at all.

T-Dates work the other way round. Add the dates you already care about — when a team actually plays, when a market is actually open — and everything else disappears. It is the fastest way to ask "does anything land here?"

Seven

What this is not

Ophis is arithmetic on the dates you supply. It projects intervals forward and ranks the results by how many independent operations and number-patterns agree. It does not know what your event was, and it has no access to anything beyond the dates in front of it.

The author was candid that the method eliminates rather than predicts: it "has ELIMINATED all future dates that bear no relativity to X-1 and X-2", leaving a shortlist on which "one, two or three of the projections will mark a future date when a similar event will occur again." A shortlist is what you get.

Treat it as a study instrument and a way of interrogating a calendar — which is what makes the Audit screen the most useful one in the application. Every number on every row can be traced back to the arithmetic that produced it. Click any result to see it.

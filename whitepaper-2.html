After Parity: What the Ophis Rebuild Became

A second white paper on natori-on-psyfr: the instrument, the readings, and the contract that held

Version 3.0 · November 2026

The first white paper (v2.0, August 2026) showed how a closed prediction engine was torn down and rebuilt bit-exact. This one picks up where that one stopped. Since then the rebuild has grown from a verified copy of the original into an instrument people actually use, and everything added on top of the engine has been held to the same rule: it does not move a number.

Abstract

Ophis is a date-projection instrument by Jason M. Breshears. You give it dates on which something similar happened. It counts the whole days between every pair of them, sends each count through a table of arithmetic operations, and projects dates forward. Then it scores those projections against a resonance table, the MSRF. The original shipped as a 100 MB Windows Electron program. In August 2026 this project finished rebuilding it as static browser files that agree with the original on every number it produces.

The question after August was what to do with a rebuild that is exact. In the ten weeks since, seventy commits have answered it in three directions:

  1. The operator's side. Protocol Prime is now a real switch: Objective or Subjective. The author's operating procedure (SOP) shows up as warnings that never block a cast. A newcomer gets a guided first run, and the operation table now defaults to 36 operations instead of 16.
  2. The reader's side. The author's later design documents are built in as readings: optional tags, a validity rule, a column for every anchor, and moon cycles. Each one reads a finished run and changes no score, no rank and no row.
  3. The file's side. Files move in both directions between the rebuild and the original desktop program. The rebuild opens what the original saves, and Save for Ophis v12 writes what the original opens. The worked examples now ship in both formats.

The engine's parity suite rose from 134 assertions to 140 and still passes. Seven more suites, kept out of that count on purpose, pin the new layers. The general result carries over from the first paper with a sharper edge. Once an engine is pinned, you can build almost anything around it, provided each addition can show it leaves the engine's output alone. That proof is the price of admission, and it is cheap when the engine is a pure function.

Keywords: reimplementation, parity testing, non-interfering extensions, design-document recovery, file-format compatibility, honest instruments.

1. What it is

The core loop is the same one the first paper described:

StepWhat happens
X-DatesYou enter two or more dates when something similar happened
YFor every pair of dates, the whole number of days between them. N dates give N(N−1)/2 pairs
OperationsEach operation turns Y into an offset from one of the two dates: X2+oph_round(Y), X1+Y/OPH_PHI, and so on
Z-DatesEach offset lands on a day. Different operations that land on the same day are merged into one row
ScoringEach row scores by how many operations hit it, their weights, and whether its day count from its anchor is in the MSRF table
FiltersNine output filters trim the list. By default they hide the past and anything more than 2,559 days past the last X-Date

None of that changed. What changed is everything around it.

Framing, carried forward verbatim from the licence: Ophis is a study instrument. It renders the Archaix thesis of Jason M. Breshears as arithmetic on the dates you supply. It knows nothing about your event. Where this paper says a date "resonates", it means the program's scoring function gave it points, nothing more.

2. The contract, stated

The first paper's method was to treat the original as a live oracle and pin the rebuild to its output. That method became a standing rule for the repository:

The maths is a contract, not an implementation detail. A failing parity test means the rebuild drifted. It never means the fixture needs updating.

Every addition since August had to answer one question before it could ship: show me that it doesn't move the engine. There are three ways to pass:

Kind of additionHow it proves non-interference
Reads a finished run (tags, Lab readings, windows view)A test runs the engine, applies the reading, and asserts that every row, score, hit count and rank is unchanged
Changes inputs, not maths (Protocol Prime, the after-sunset box)The engine is untouched. The test pins what goes in, and the parity suite pins what the engine does with it
Changes a default (36 operations)The engine's own default stays the shipped 16. Only new documents start with 36, and a .oph that names its operations is cast exactly as written

The table of traps from the handoff is still in force: OPH_PI = 3.14, the epsilon-free Vortex tolerance, and round1(-1.25) === -1.2. They look like bugs and they are pinned behaviour. None of the seventy commits "fixed" one.

3. Input: Protocol Prime becomes a switch

The first paper's biggest finding was something the software never mentions. The author's flow chart takes three controls, and the third one, Protocol Prime, is the date the projection is being run. Leave it out and you are running a third of the method without knowing it.

A second set of the author's documents (studied in reverse/23) turned that from a reconstruction into a rule with a scope:

KindWhat the X-Dates areRule
ObjectiveDates not tied to the person asking: market moves, a team's resultsNo extra date
SubjectiveThe operator's or a client's own datesToday must be the last X-Date

The rebuild now has an Objective / Subjective switch on each event. Choosing Subjective adds today in date order, using the same code as the ☉ Today button. The author also said, in a recorded tutorial, that Protocol Prime is only the day of asking. So when a subjective event is re-run on a later day, the old Protocol Prime date is removed and replaced, never kept alongside the new one. It was never a past occurrence of the event.

The event remembers which X-Date it added, in a protocol_prime_date field. That field is written to the file only for subjective events. Absent means Objective, so every older file, and every shipped example, stays byte-identical. The edge cases are pinned. A PP date you have since retyped is yours and is left alone. A file from before this change never has an earlier date guessed to be PP. And PP never moves backwards when the clock is set back.

3.1 The SOP, as advice

The author's standard operating procedure sets limits the original never enforced. They now appear as warnings under the X-Dates, and none of them blocks a cast:

  • the first two dates are under 6 days apart;
  • an X-Date is more than 3 years old (the SOP says to use 5 or more dates and read the results as windows, not days);
  • a subjective event's last date is not today.

Since the SOP says older casts should be read as periods, the results bar gained Rows: Days | 7-day windows. A window opens at a Z-Date and takes in every Z-Date less than seven days later. It shows summed hits and the best single score, and re-scores nothing.

3.2 A newcomer's door

start.html (Start here) is a guided first run told as a short story. You name something that keeps happening, add the dates, and see which days the patterns point to. It reads the same engine and shows only rows runOphis produced. It adds two helpers from the author's later notes:

  • an After sunset box for each date. Ophis counts days by the Earth's rotation and the day turns at sunset, so an evening event belongs to the next day. The full app's HH:MM scope remains the exact version.
  • a how far ahead to look tip, from the author's brief to his programmer. For example, controls 5 days apart point about a month out.

The results screen can also hand your dates to a separate 3D Pyramid Chronograph. The dates travel in the URL fragment, which never reaches a server.

4. The table grows: 16 → 36 operations

The original's v12 table runs sixteen operations. Two additive packs sit beside it:

  • Extras (17–26): ten operations built on the author's other constants (e, 1.38, 5.52, 2.178, 0.360).
  • The Mirrors (27–36): ten operations that fill the gaps in the sixteen's grid. Several forms ran on only one anchor: the isometric and holo dates, Y/CRV, Y/PHI, (Y/2)×PHI and Y×PHI ran on X2 and never on X1. And no operation ever halved the hepta-cycle or divided by π. With the Mirrors in, every form runs on both anchors (full grid in MIRRORS.md).

Both packs are additive. Adding one appends rows and never renumbers an existing row, which matters because an operation's ordinal decides which anchor its X1+ binds to. Adding a pack twice does nothing.

New documents now start with all 36 switched on. The engine's own default did not change: a .oph with no operations still means the shipped sixteen, which is what the parity suite pins. A stored document whose table was still the untouched sixteen is brought up to 36 on restore, and an edited table is left alone.

What 36 does to a run, measured on the worked example (five dates, ten pairs, clock-free):

16 operations36 operations
Projections160360
Rows shown114200
Rows hidden by filters39112
Top row09/29/2027 · score 3 · 4 hits10/05/2027 · score 5 · 4 hits
Rows that pass the author's validity rule (§5.2)7496

More operations means more chances to collide, so scores rise and the top row moves. Neither table is "right". They are two settings of the instrument, and the file records which one a run used.

5. The centre: readings that change no score

This is the largest body of new work, and the most disciplined. Two more sets of the author's design documents (studied in reverse/23 and reverse/24) described filters and rules the shipped program never ran. Wherever a document gave enough to compute, the rule was built as a reading: off by default, applied to a finished run, and proved by test to leave every score and rank as it was.

5.1 Optional tags (Settings → S3)

Dashed pills in a Tags column, kept visibly apart from the MSRF chips. The caption says they are "the author's list, not in the shipped MSRF".

TagSourceRule
FibonacciOther MSRA FiltersF = {8, 13, 21 … 1597}, projected forward from the anchors. A member of F that lands on a Core Algorithm date marks that date a Fibonacci Projection
Geo (138)Geodetic ChronometryMultiples of 138 up to 2484, and 365.25 × 1.38k (504.0 … 2520.2)
DiurDiurnal ProjectionsThe 365.25 × and ÷ series for 1.618 and 3.14, up to (365.25 ÷ 3.14) × 22 = 2559.08
HoloHoloflective Sums75 sum/reversal pairs (12 ⇄ 21, 405 ⇄ 504 …), plus 2178, "the Immortal Loop Number"

A Z-Date is tagged when an operation's day count from its anchor is within 0.1 day of a list value, which is one display step of the count. The lists are generated from the author's rules, not typed in, and the Holo test checks that every pair really is a digit reversal. That also guards the transcription.

One result changed the record. The first study concluded that the 2,559-day default window and the author's "2556-day period" had collided by accident. Diurnal Projections lists both: 365.25 × 7 = 2556.75, and (365.25 ÷ 3.14) × 22 = 2559.08. So the default and the MSRF ceiling share a source, and the earlier conclusion was amended.

5.2 The Lab

A screen of readings from the author's brief to his original programmer:

Lab panelWhat it shows
L1 · Author's validity ruleBeta dates need a second beta hit or an MSRF match. ALPHA dates stand alone. The isometric and holo projections count only with an MSRF match. On the worked example it keeps 74 of 114 rows. The results table sets the rest aside and says how many, while the run, the chart and the exports keep every row
L2 · How far ahead to lookThe author's look-ahead rule, read from your latest date and closest pair
L3 · Every anchor's countThe brief asks for "a column per X-date". The engine probes one count per projection; this shows the MSRF tier of the count from every anchor. On the worked example, the top row is 405 days from X2 (Tier III) and 204 from X3 (Tier II), though neither projected it
L4 · Gaps and the moonEach Y in lunations, and any Lunation, Saros, Metonic, Exeligmos or Callippic return within a day
L5 · The moon's long returnsThe Antikythera mechanism's back dials, started on your first date
L6 · What cannot be builtThe Hydrogen Spacing Angle and Holofractal Recognition subroutines, described in prose with no arithmetic. The screen says so instead of inventing a rule

L6 is the part worth copying. A rebuild that fills a gap in its source with a plausible guess has stopped being a rebuild. Saying "not computable from what is written" on screen is part of the instrument's honesty.

5.3 What the evidence says

A users' group study, On Scaling Vectors, ran the engine on about 26 million graded sports predictions with multiple-comparison correction. Individual MSRF numbers showed no enrichment over the base rate. Of more than 162,000 number pairs, none survived false-discovery-rate correction. Its conclusion is that the engine runs saturated, and any signal lies in the composite score, not in which numbers fire. This project's own backtest on personal test sets also found no edge over chance. Both results are reported here because an instrument that adds readings has a duty to say what the readings have and haven't been shown to do.

6. Output: the round trip to the original

The rebuild writes its own .oph format, v13, with X-Dates as {y, m, d}. The original v12 desktop program reads only {date: "MM/DD/YYYY"}, and in its default loose mode it silently drops every date it can't parse. The event opens with no anchors and casts nothing, with no error shown.

Both directions now work:

  • In. The rebuild opens v12 files. This was found the hard way. The original, published on the same site under ophis/, writes its state to a browser storage key the rebuild also reads. Storage is scoped to an origin, not a path, so opening the original broke the rebuild: every anchor came out NaN and the run returned zero rows with an empty error list. Everything restored now goes through the same normaliser as an opened file.
  • Out. Export → Save for Ophis v12 writes app_version: "12", no schema field, v12's date form, and each event's keys in exactly the order v12's own createNewIsoEvent writes them, so a diff against a file the desktop app saved lines up field for field. Anything v12 cannot represent is listed, never silently dropped: a sort it lacks, an event type, the Subjective label, the Protocol Prime record.
  • Examples in both formats. The five worked examples ship as v13 in examples/ and as v12 copies in examples/v12/. A generator (npm run examples:v12) builds the copies with the same writer, and a test regenerates them in memory and fails if a committed copy is stale or out of v12's exact key order.

The original program itself is also in the repository now, under ophis/. It is the renderer of Ophis_v12_Windows.exe copied byte for byte, with a small bridge standing in for Electron, so it runs in a browser next to the rebuild that reproduces it.

Two lessons from this layer generalise.

Generate the copy; don't maintain it. Two hand-made single-file builds had drifted apart and kept a fixed defect alive in both until each was patched by hand. The bundler exists so that can't happen again. A plain concatenation would have been worse than nothing: the modules collide on names (hit, span, COLUMNS), so pasting them together gives a file that parses and then misbehaves.

Say which lens you are looking through. Users read the PSYFR Oracle as if it were v12. The fix was not a code change but a sentence at the top of the page, plus a fold listing the differences in plain words.

The parity count grew from 134 to 140. Everything built on top of the engine is pinned in the side suites instead. That way the headline number still means one thing: agreement with the original. CI also still forbids eval, new Function and every markup sink in src/, and fails a stale single-file build.

The repaired defects are worth a line each, because each was invisible in the source and obvious in use:

  • The stuck document. An append bug saved anchors out of order in people's browsers. Uploading a fixed build couldn't reach them. Restore now repairs only events that cannot cast as they stand. Index order is load-bearing, so re-ordering a working document would change its results, and that is not allowed.
  • The wrong diagnosis. "No results. You probably have to loosen up a filter" sent a user to the filters for an hour when the real cause was three T-Dates. The empty panel now names the actual cause, and Turn off every filter turns off all eight, not four.
  • A second eclipse table. The rebuild now carries a corrected eclipse table built from NASA's canon, alongside the original's table, which has four documented defects. The original stays the default, because Chronicon scores eclipses and switching tables would change its numbers. That choice belongs to the reader, made knowingly.

9. What it is not

Ophis performs arithmetic on the dates it is given and ranks the results by how many independent operations and number patterns agree. Since November, every results screen says the rest in the users' group's own words:

Ophis is a prediction tool, not a betting tool. How you use its dates is your decision and your responsibility; there are no guarantees. Never wager money you and your family can't live without.

The evidence in §5.3 is why that line is there. The rebuild is exact about what the original computes. It makes no claim, and no test in this repository makes one, that what it computes predicts anything.

Coda: 19, 138, and a mirror

The repository's fourth example, 04-even-spacing-19-138.oph, is three dates 138 days apart starting on the 19th. It scores 4.5, higher than the app's own worked example, and the manual explains why that proves nothing. The gaps are 138 and 276. Half of 276 is 138, so two different formulas both compute 138 × 1.618 = 223 from the same date and land on the same day. The pattern was typed in, not found.

Two numbers in that sentence turn up elsewhere in this paper. That is a coincidence, recorded as one:

  • 223 is also the Saros: 223 lunations, after which eclipses repeat (Lab L4).
  • 19 years is the Metonic cycle, 235 lunations, after which the moon's phases fall on the same calendar dates again. And 138 is the Geodetic Chronometry base the Geo tag counts in.

This paper is built as a mirror, after the operation the author called holoflective. Its nine sections pair from the outside in: what it is ⇄ what it is not (1 ⇄ 9), the contract stated ⇄ kept (2 ⇄ 8), the operator's door ⇄ the reader's doors (3 ⇄ 7), the table that grew ⇄ the files that carry it (4 ⇄ 6). Section 5, the readings that change nothing, sits alone at the centre, the way a palindrome's middle letter has no partner. Like the 19/138 example, the symmetry was put there on purpose, and it proves nothing about the subject.
